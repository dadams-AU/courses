The official answer is a line

  • 1965: Mollie Orshansky, a bureaucrat at the Social Security Administration.
  • A bare-bones food budget was about a third of a family’s spending — so: cheap food budget × 3 = the poverty line.
  • Updated for inflation and nothing else, ever since.
  • 2026: $15,960 for one person · $33,000 for a family of four.

Plenty of poverty above the line

  • What do you call raising two kids on $50,000 in Miami?
  • Or here: in Orange County, the official “low income” line for a family of four is $148,850 (2026) — four and a half times the poverty line.
  • Rent takes half the paycheck; student loans take a quarter.
  • Income volatility — paychecks swinging month to month — has doubled since 1970.
  • Below the line one month, just above it the next, never steady.

Crystal

  • Born premature, the day her mother was stabbed. Foster care at five. Twenty-five placements by eighteen.
  • At eighteen: SSI, $754 a month. “SSI always come.”
  • The adult reevaluation said otherwise. Then shelters, streets, survival.

The knot

Desmond’s image for poverty as it is lived: several hardships tangled together, each pulling the others tighter, while the line measures only income.

  • Pain — two amputations a week in America’s meatpacking plants; free-Advil vending machines in the warehouse.
  • Instability — 3.6 million eviction filings in an average year.
  • Fear — half of all new jobs gone within a year; wages wobbly week to week.
  • Lost liberty — almost 2 million people in prison and jail today, uncounted by the poverty statistics.
  • Shame — “poverty felt morally.” There is no flag for poor rights.
  • The bandwidth tax — scarcity takes more mind than a full night without sleep.

The knot has faces you’ve met

Growing Up Poor in America, Part 1 — three Ohio families, assigned last week

  • Kyah, 14, Columbus — a year without a home; blankets laid out each night into “a little twin-size bed.” “I don’t think I should have to carry these worries as a kid, but I just choose to.”
  • Fantasy, Marietta — gas station wages: “Some weeks I’m paycheck to paycheck. Most weeks I’m paycheck till the next day.”
  • Crystal, The Plains — “What I get is $485 per month and I get approximately $400 in food stamp assistance. So I take $885 times 12 equals $10,620 for three.” The 2020 poverty line for three: about $20,600.

And far below the line

  • Deep poverty: half the poverty line — $7,980 for one person, $16,500 for a family of four (2026).
  • One in twenty Americans — 16.8 million people (2024). Children fare worst: 6.2 percent, about 4.5 million, a larger share than any adult age group.
  • Angus Deaton, 2018: 5.3 million Americans poor “by global standards” — living on $4 a day or less.
    • The standard: the World Bank’s global destitution line — $1.90 a day at the time — adjusted up to about $4 for what bare survival costs in a rich country.

Poverty is no equalizer

  • There is no U.S. metro area where poor white families are concentrated in neighborhoods with poverty above 40 percent. Many poor Black and Hispanic families live exactly there (Desmond, ch. 1).
  • Median household wealth, 2022: $285,000 white · $61,600 Hispanic · $44,900 Black (Federal Reserve, Survey of Consumer Finances).
  • Roughly six white dollars for every Black one — and the dollar gap grew even as every group’s median rose.
  • Same poverty line. Different poverty.

“Poverty isn’t a line. It’s a tight knot of social maladies.”

— Desmond, chapter 1

If he’s right, what would progress even look like?

  • If poverty is a line, progress is easy to see: fewer people below it each year.
  • If poverty is a knot, a family’s income can clear the line while the knot stays tied — the statistic improves, the life doesn’t.
  • Wednesday’s reading takes up exactly that. Chapter 2’s title: “Why Haven’t We Made More Progress?”
