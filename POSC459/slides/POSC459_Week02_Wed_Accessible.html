What the record says

  • Everyone got the same tool. Every student, faculty, and staff member on 23 campuses; 675,000 users under the renewal.
  • Most of you already had it. 84% of CSU students said they used ChatGPT before the deal; 95% used some AI tool. 82% worry AI will hurt their job prospects. (CSU survey, 2025)
  • Training didn’t come with it. 67% of students say their professors don’t teach them how to use AI; 52% of faculty say it has hurt their teaching. (CalMatters)
  • It was paid for during a $2.3B budget gap. “We are cutting programs. We are laying off faculty … and we’re giving it to the richest technology companies in the world.” (a CSU sociology professor, LAist)
  • Who was in the room: about three faculty on a committee of two dozen. The student association: “We were not consulted … we weren’t even given a heads up.” (LAist; CalMatters)

Monday I left you a question

If poverty is a knot and not a line — what number would tell us we’re making progress?

Before the number: the map. Howard’s chapter is about which programs we count, and how we sort them.

Howard’s map: the two tiers

Upper tier — “social insurance” Lower tier — “welfare”
Social Security · Medicare · Disability Insurance · Unemployment Insurance · Workers’ compensation Medicaid · the EITC · SSI · TANF · Food Stamps (SNAP) · Public housing and Section 8

The textbook story: the upper tier is larger, more generous, more uniform, more popular, and harder to cut. The lower tier is the one Americans say they hate.

Which of these has someone in this room, or their family, used?

Cracks in the tiers

  • Same family, same program, same year. TANF, family of three, 2002: $201 a month in Texas, $636 in Connecticut.
  • Same hand. Lose it on the job: up to $53,500 in Indiana, $181,700 across the line in Illinois. Workers’ comp is upper tier.
  • Unemployment insurance reached fewer than 40 percent of the unemployed in 2000. Upper tier.
  • Medicaid spent about $10,000 per elderly patient in 2001. Medicare: about $6,000. The lower tier outspends the upper.
  • Americans hate “welfare.” Ask about SSI, Medicaid, or food for the poor, and support runs about where Social Security’s does (Gilens).

Howard’s rule: if one of the Big Three doesn’t fit the model, build a better model. Verdict: reject the tiers.

Desmond’s puzzle: the flatline

1970 1990 2010 2019
12.6% 13.5% 15.1% 10.5%

The official poverty rate, over fifty years. It never left that band.

Over the same years, spending on the thirteen largest anti-poverty programs — per American, inflation-adjusted — went from $1,015 (1980) to $3,419 (2017). More than three times the money.

Three times the spending per person. The same share of people poor. Why?

Desmond’s suspects: we got stingy · immigrants · single parents. He tests each. Each fails.

But which number was that?

The official measure never sees SNAP, the EITC, housing help, or a stimulus check. Count those — the Supplemental Poverty Measure does — and poverty falls meaningfully over the same years.

Fantasy, in the film: “I have received extra food stamps cause of COVID. That stimulus, that paid half my bills.”

The official measure records her as untouched.

Where the dollars leak

  • 22 cents of every budgeted TANF dollar reached families as cash in 2020. About $6B sat unspent.
  • Becky, in the film — approved for pandemic unemployment: “Everything is still pending, though, so I haven’t gotten anything yet.”
  • Crystal — “I am still required to work my hours for my assistance … maybe my assistance could get shut down.”

And Howard’s map: Crystal’s household runs on food stamps, HUD, disability-linked cash, and free school lunch at once — four programs, both “tiers,” one trailer.

Two books, one question

  • Howard: the map you were handed doesn’t describe the territory.
  • Desmond: the money on the map doesn’t all arrive.

So when Americans say they hate “welfare” — which program are they picturing? Is it even on Howard’s map?
