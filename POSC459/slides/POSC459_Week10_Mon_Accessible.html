1. The broken system

“So You Think You’re Insured? (Think Again)” Time, March 16, 2009

Pat Tumulty, San Antonio. Six years without job-based coverage. Six-month Assurant policies, high deductible. Kidney failure. “Preexisting condition.” Claim denied. Hospital’s highest rates. Texas Medicaid: adults with children, 26% of poverty.

Jacobs and Skocpol, pp. 1–2

How it came to this

  • 1880s–1945: other rich countries go universal; the US tries and fails
  • 1940s: Truman loses. Hospitals, research, and patents get funded instead
  • Unions and big employers buy tax-subsidized coverage; the “haves” no longer need the “have-nots”
  • 1965: Medicare and Medicaid fill the gaps private insurers won’t touch
  • Late 1970s on: costs explode; a million more uninsured every year
  • 2000–2007: public programs stall; SCHIP vetoed; Part D unpaid for

Jacobs and Skocpol, pp. 5–8

2. The window: 2008

  • Las Vegas forum, March 24, 2007: Clinton and Edwards have plans. Obama “could wing it”
  • Obama’s plan: October 2007. No individual mandate. “Affordability”
  • Clinton: his plan “simply flunks the test” on universal coverage
  • June 2008: Bristol, Virginia, the first general-election event. Health care, by story
  • October 2008: Obama attacks McCain for “taxing health benefits for the first time ever”

Jacobs and Skocpol, pp. 10–14

Why it passed: the standard answer

Democratic president + Democratic Congress, 2006 and 2008

Fewer Jim Coopers: a more homogeneous caucus

Necessary. Not sufficient.

“Why this approach? Why this session? Why did Obama push forward given how much else was on his plate?”

Hacker, pp. 863–864

Why it passed: three factors

Factor 1993–94 2009–10
Economic context Short 1991 recession Deep downturn; 2-to-1 said “take it on right now”
Group conflict Harry and Louise 1.0: “There has to be a better way” Harry and Louise 2.0: “It’s about time”
Democratic coalescence Single payer vs. play-or-pay vs. incrementalism One plan, the Massachusetts template

Hacker, pp. 864–867

The groups switched sides

  • Industry was being priced out of its own customers; fewer insured meant fewer paying patients
  • AHIP: regulation in exchange for a coverage requirement, the Massachusetts deal
  • PhRMA and hospitals: early deals; modest givebacks, White House protection
  • AMA: endorsed both bills, waiting on the Medicare “doc fix”
  • Lobbying, 1998–2010: $4 billion; 2009 alone: $500 million+

The quid pro quo: more public regulation and financing in return for guaranteed customers

Hacker, pp. 864–865

Democrats sang from one hymnbook

  • Massachusetts 2006: mandate + Medicaid expansion + exchanges
  • Plus: stronger cost controls, employer requirements, a public option
  • HCAN: unions, think tanks, advocacy groups; a pledge list, 190 members of Congress
  • Modeled on Americans for Tax Reform’s no-new-taxes pledge
  • The lesson of 1994: “one shot when the window opened”

Hacker, pp. 866–867

Why so limited: the plan itself

“It incorporates all sorts of Republican ideas.” — Barack Obama, Today, March 2010

  • Romney’s 2006 Massachusetts law, shaped by the Heritage Foundation
  • Close to the 1993 moderate-Republican alternative to Clinton
  • The “Obama-Romney-McCain Health Plan”

Roots: the employer-based path, and Medicare 1965, which covered the sympathetic and left the rest

Hacker, p. 867

What the House passed, and the Senate killed

Provision House bill Senate bill Final law
Public option ✔ ✘ ✘
National exchange ✔ state exchanges state exchanges
Tough employer requirement ✔ weak weak

“What stood in the way of the public option… was less Joe Lieberman than a once-obscure procedural rule.”

Hacker, pp. 868–869

Why so limited: the CBO

Three fights the White House picked, against public opinion:

  1. The individual mandate
  2. The tax on high-cost plans
  3. An independent Medicare commission

Reason: the CBO score. The mandate kept the exchanges from becoming a dumping ground; the plan tax scored as savings.

The public option would have saved ~$150 billion over ten years. It faced the fiercest group resistance, so it went.

Hacker, pp. 869–870

Why so angry

“American politics has often been an arena for angry minds.” — Hofstadter, 1964

  • The provisions polled well. “The health care bill” polled at ~50% or less
  • Only about half knew the popular provisions were in it
  • Tea Party supporters: slightly upscale, very conservative Republicans
  • Asymmetric polarization: the GOP moved right; moderates lost first
  • Zero Republican votes for the final bill, either chamber

Hacker, pp. 870–872

Was it worth it?

Harkin: a “starter home”

Social Security’s “cruel hoax” to “third rail” took decades, in a friendlier fiscal climate, with a simpler program

No tangible benefits for years. A complex statute to implement and defend.

“Policy substance matters.”

Hacker, p. 872; Lasswell: who gets what, when, how
