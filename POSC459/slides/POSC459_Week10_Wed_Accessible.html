The story in one slide: February 2009 to March 2010

When What happened
Feb.–Mar. 2009 Obama asks Congress for health reform and holds a White House summit with both parties and the industry
Spring–summer 2009 Five congressional committees write bills; the White House cuts deals with hospitals, doctors, and drug makers
August 2009 Congress goes home; angry Tea Party crowds take over members’ town-hall meetings
Sept. 9, 2009 Obama addresses Congress: “I am determined to be the last” president to take this up
Nov. 7, 2009 The House passes its bill, 220–215
Dec. 24, 2009 The Senate passes a different bill, 60–39, with exactly the 60 votes it needs
Jan. 19, 2010 Republican Scott Brown wins a Massachusetts Senate seat. Democrats now have 59. Reform looks dead
Mar. 21–23, 2010 The House passes the Senate bill plus a package of fixes, 219–212. Obama signs the Affordable Care Act

Jacobs and Skocpol, ch. 2, pp. 1–4; ch. 3, pp. 1–2

Words you’ll need today

Term Plain meaning
Filibuster / cloture In the Senate, any 41 senators can block a bill by refusing to end debate. Ending debate (“cloture”) takes 60 votes. The House has no such rule
CBO score The Congressional Budget Office’s official estimate of what a bill costs over ten years. Members often won’t vote for a bill that “scores” badly
Individual mandate A requirement that everyone carry health insurance or pay a penalty
Exchange A marketplace where people without job coverage shop for private plans, with government subsidies to help pay
Public option A government-run insurance plan, like Medicare, that would compete with private plans on the exchange
“Cadillac tax” A tax on very expensive employer health plans
Reconciliation A budget procedure that needs only 51 Senate votes, but can change only taxes and spending, not rules

1. Obama let Congress write the bill, because Clinton hadn’t

What happened: In 1993 President Clinton had a task force of 500+ experts spend nine months writing a detailed plan and sent it to Congress. It never got out of committee, and Democrats lost Congress in 1994. Obama did the opposite: he set broad goals and left the writing to the committees.

Clinton, 1993 Obama, 2009
Who wrote the bill A White House task force Five congressional committees
Result No bill passed either chamber Both chambers passed bills in under a year
The cost A big, early target for opponents Missed deadlines; supporters unsure what he wanted

The point: A president can’t order Congress to pass a domestic law. Obama traded control over the details for a better chance of getting any bill at all.

Jacobs and Skocpol, ch. 2, pp. 2–6

Pelosi needed a majority; Reid needed every single Democrat

Who they are: Nancy Pelosi was Speaker of the House. Harry Reid was the Senate’s Democratic leader.

House: Pelosi Senate: Reid
Votes needed A simple majority, 218 60, to stop a filibuster
What she/he had 255 Democrats, 75 more than Republicans 58 Democrats + 2 independents = exactly 60
What that meant Could lose dozens of Democrats and still win Could lose no one

The point: With exactly 60, every Democratic senator was the deciding vote, so each could demand a price. Two did: extra Medicaid money for Nebraska (Ben Nelson) and for Louisiana (Mary Landrieu).

Jacobs and Skocpol, ch. 2, pp. 1–2, 6–8

Why liberals had the least bargaining power

  • Moderate Democrats could walk away from reform, and some would gain votes at home by doing it
  • Liberal Democrats wanted a bill more than anyone, so they would give things up to save it
  • Everyone knew this, including Max Baucus, chair of the Senate Finance Committee, who wrote the Senate’s bill

The point: In a negotiation, the side that wants the deal most has the least power over its terms. That is why the Senate bill leaned toward the moderates.

Jacobs and Skocpol, ch. 2, pp. 8–9

The budget scorekeepers had a veto too

  • Moderate Democrats would not vote for a bill unless the CBO said it didn’t add to the deficit
  • Democrats prepared for years: Peter Orszag built up CBO’s health staff, then became Obama’s budget director
  • CBO stayed independent and would not give private previews
  • “That’s what it’s going to say on my tombstone: ‘He was waiting for C.B.O.’” — Senator Dick Durbin

The point: Provisions that “scored” well got into the bill even when the public disliked them, like the mandate and the Cadillac tax. Remember this at the end of class.

Jacobs and Skocpol, ch. 2, pp. 10–11

2. The deals: the industry gave a little to gain a lot

What happened: The White House and Senate Finance negotiated with each health industry in private. The pitch: reform brings 32 million new paying customers. In return, give back some money and don’t attack the bill.

Group What it gave up What it got
Hospitals (American Hospital Assn.) $155 billion in future Medicare payments ~$171 billion from newly insured patients
Doctors (American Medical Assn.) ~$80 billion in payment cuts ~$228 billion; it backed even the House bill
Drug makers (PhRMA) $85 billion in discounts and fees New customers; no imports of cheaper foreign drugs
Insurers (AHIP) Accepted new rules on how they sell insurance The mandate: everyone must buy coverage

The point: In 1993–94 these groups killed Clinton’s plan. In 2009 most of them stayed quiet or paid for ads supporting reform. The White House’s rule: “no peace, no reform.”

Jacobs and Skocpol, ch. 2, pp. 11–16

Why the insurers turned against the bill: too little government

What happened: The insurers negotiated all year. Then in September 2009 the Senate Finance Committee, under Republican pressure, cut the penalty for not buying insurance.

  • New rules would force insurers to sell to sick people. With a weak penalty, healthy people could wait to buy coverage until they got sick.
  • So the insurers’ trade group (AHIP) paid PricewaterhouseCoopers for a study predicting soaring premiums. The study left out the subsidies, on AHIP’s instructions.
  • The White House attacked the study within a day; PwC backed away; the rules on insurers got tougher.

The point: Insurers ran ads against a “government takeover,” but what pushed them into open opposition was a mandate too weak to deliver healthy customers.

Vote: were the deals corruption, or how laws get made?

Jacobs and Skocpol, ch. 2, pp. 14–16

3. Pressure from the right: the Tea Party

Who they were: about 25% of adults; older, richer, and whiter than the country; the most conservative part of the Republican base. 90% disapproved of Obama on health care (the public overall: 51%).

  • August 2009: activists packed members’ town halls. Sean Hannity’s website: “Become Party of the mob!”
  • Sept. 9: Republican Rep. Joe Wilson shouted “You lie!” during Obama’s speech to Congress
  • Many protesters were on Medicare, which they saw as earned. Coverage for younger, poorer people looked like “handouts”

The point: The Tea Party’s power over Congress ran through Republican primaries. Any Republican who bargained with Democrats risked losing to a challenger from the right.

Jacobs and Skocpol, ch. 2, pp. 17–18, 21

Pressure from the left: the fight for a public option

What happened: Liberals had already given up on single payer. Their compromise was the public option, a government plan to compete with private insurers. A coalition of 1,000+ groups (Health Care for America Now) pushed for it, and polls showed majorities in favor.

  • The House passed a weak version that would have covered only 2% of the insured, 3–4 million people
  • In the Senate it never came close to 60; Jacobs and Skocpol count 52 at best
  • A substitute, letting people 55–64 buy into Medicare, died when Senator Joe Lieberman refused at the last minute

The point: The left lost the public option but used it as a bargaining chip. In exchange for dropping it, liberals won larger subsidies and higher taxes on the rich.

Jacobs and Skocpol, ch. 2, pp. 18–21

4. Why no Republican voted for it

What happened: All summer, Max Baucus negotiated with a “Gang of Six” senators, three Democrats and three Republicans (Chuck Grassley, Mike Enzi, Olympia Snowe). They met 31 times.

  • Grassley himself put the mandate, Medicare savings, and subsidies on the table
  • By late summer he was warning of “death panels,” a false claim about a provision paying doctors for voluntary end-of-life counseling
  • In October one Republican, Snowe, voted for the Finance bill. On final passage: zero Republicans in either chamber

The point: Social Security and Medicare both passed with Republican votes. No major social law since the New Deal had passed with none.

Jacobs and Skocpol, ch. 2, pp. 21–23

The Republican strategy was unanimous opposition

“If we’re able to stop Obama on this, it will be his Waterloo. It will break him.” — Senator Jim DeMint, July 2009

Mitch McConnell, the Senate Republican leader, told his members it was “absolutely critical that everybody be together,” so Democrats could never call the bill bipartisan.

The point: The bill was full of Republican ideas (the mandate and exchanges came from Mitt Romney’s Massachusetts law). Republicans opposed it anyway, betting it would win them the 2010 election, as killing Clinton’s plan had in 1994.

Jacobs and Skocpol, ch. 2, pp. 23–24

5. Two bills, two ways to pay

What happened: There was never an “Obama plan.” Obama set four goals (cover more people, regulate insurers, pay for it, slow costs), and each chamber wrote its own bill.

Issue House bill, Nov. 7 Senate bill, Dec. 24
How to pay Tax incomes over $1 million Cadillac tax on expensive job-based plans
Employers Firms of 50+ must provide coverage or pay Much weaker
Exchange One national exchange 50 state exchanges
Public option A weak one None
Medicare cost commission No Yes
Vote 220–215 60–39

The point: The House taxed the rich and employers. The Senate cut costs. In January the two had to be merged.

Jacobs and Skocpol, ch. 2, pp. 25–32

Side fights that nearly sank it

  • Abortion: the House bill passed only after Rep. Bart Stupak won an amendment restricting abortion coverage
  • Immigrants: could undocumented people buy insurance with their own money? Compromised toward no
  • Medicare: cuts to private Medicare Advantage insurers were sold as cuts to all seniors; three-quarters weren’t in those plans
  • State deals: the “Cornhusker Kickback” for Nebraska, the “Louisiana Purchase”

Jacobs and Skocpol, ch. 2, pp. 1–2, 27, 32

6. January 19, 2010: Democrats lose their 60th vote

What happened: Ted Kennedy, the Senate’s leading health reformer, died in August 2009. In the special election for his Massachusetts seat, Republican Scott Brown beat Democrat Martha Coakley. Brown ran as the 41st vote, the one that would let Republicans filibuster the bill.

  • Massachusetts had zero Republicans in Congress, and 8 in 10 voters there liked their own state’s version of this reform
  • Union households voted for Brown, 49–46, angry about the Cadillac tax on their health plans
  • “ObamaCare went into the emergency room in Massachusetts and didn’t make it out alive.” — Fred Barnes, Weekly Standard

The point: The House and Senate bills agreed on 85% of their provisions, but they still had to be merged and passed again, and the Senate could no longer reach 60.

Jacobs and Skocpol, ch. 3, pp. 1–4

You are Nancy Pelosi. January 20, 2010.

What you know: The Senate passed its bill, but can’t pass a new one. The Senate bill includes the Cornhusker Kickback, the Cadillac tax unions hate, state exchanges, and abortion language your pro-choice members resent. Every House member faces voters in nine months.

Option Vote you lose Vote you need
A. Rush a merged bill through the Senate before Brown is sworn in
B. Have the House pass the Senate’s bill exactly as is
C. Shrink it: children’s coverage and a few insurer rules
D. Something else. Say what.

Five weeks of panic, then a restart

  • Democrats froze. Rep. Barney Frank said reform was dead; the White House said little
  • February: Anthem Blue Cross announced a 39% rate increase in California, a reminder of what unregulated insurance looked like
  • Obama finally put out his own plan (Feb. 22) and held a seven-hour televised summit with Republicans at Blair House (Feb. 25). No Republican votes came of it

Jacobs and Skocpol, ch. 3, pp. 4–9

The solution: the Senate bill plus a “sidecar”

What happened: The House passed the Senate’s bill unchanged, plus a second bill of fixes. The Senate passed the fixes by reconciliation, with 51 votes. But reconciliation can change only taxes and spending.

Could be fixed (money) Stuck as the Senate wrote it (rules)
Cornhusker Kickback removed State exchanges, not one national exchange
Cadillac tax reduced and delayed The abortion language
New 3.8% tax on investment income; new taxes on health industries Anything else that wasn’t taxes or spending
Student-loan savings of $60 billion+, about half used for the bill

The point: Losing the 60th vote strengthened the bill in places. At 51 votes, Ben Nelson’s vote no longer mattered, so Nebraska lost its deal.

Jacobs and Skocpol, ch. 3, pp. 9–12

March 21, 2010: 219–212

  • The last holdouts were Stupak’s anti-abortion Democrats; Obama promised an executive order barring federal abortion funding
  • Catholic hospitals and leaders of 60,000 Catholic nuns backed the bill; the Catholic bishops opposed it
  • House Republican leader John Boehner: “Armageddon”
  • Final count: zero Republican votes in either chamber. Obama signed it March 23

Jacobs and Skocpol, ch. 3, pp. 2, 12–13

7. What the law promised (as Jacobs and Skocpol saw it in 2015)

Share of Americans insured 83% → 92% projected; 26 million more people
Medicaid Open to all low-income adults up to 138% of poverty, not only parents and children
Exchange subsidies Help up to 4× poverty; premiums capped at 2% to 9.5% of income
New rules for insurers No denying coverage for preexisting conditions; no canceling policies when people get sick; no lifetime caps
Young adults Can stay on a parent’s plan until 26

The point: Chapter 4 argues for the law. Read it like any advocacy document: a claim, its evidence, and who is making it.

Jacobs and Skocpol, ch. 4, pp. 2–9, 14

Who pays

  • Couples earning over $250,000 (individuals, $200,000): a higher Medicare tax, 1.45% → 2.35%, plus a new 3.8% tax on investment income
  • Insurers lose $156 billion in Medicare Advantage overpayments
  • Drug, device, and insurance companies pay new fees
  • CBO: the law reduces the deficit, about $140 billion in its first ten years

“Conservative means to pursue progressive ends” (p. 8): private insurers and tax credits, with the benefits going to people of modest income

Jacobs and Skocpol, ch. 4, pp. 8–10, 13–14

The painful parts were delayed. Did they survive?

What Jacobs and Skocpol said: reformers postponed the cost controls that would hurt someone specific. Will future officials keep them? “We think they will” (p. 17).

Provision Why it was in the bill What happened later
Individual mandate penalty Kept healthy people in the pool; scored well Penalty cut to $0 from 2019 (Tax Cuts and Jobs Act, 2017)
Medicare cost commission (IPAB) Projected savings Never appointed; repealed 2018
Cadillac tax Revenue and cost control Delayed twice; repealed Dec. 2019, never collected

The point: The benefits (coverage to 26, preexisting conditions, Medicaid, subsidies) survived. The three provisions that cost someone something were all undone, and each was in the bill because it scored well with CBO.

Jacobs and Skocpol, ch. 4, pp. 15–17; “What happened later” is from outside the readings
